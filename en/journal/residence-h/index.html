Clean Is Not Surface Minimalism — It Is Complexity, Organized in Advance

Before a space can breathe, something has to disappear.

This project began with the bathroom.

Not because the bathroom mattered most, but because until its problems were solved, the logic of the shared space could not stand. The soil stack could not be moved, so once the toilet shifted the horizontal run had to find another route; along the floor would have been simplest, but it meant raising the level, and the owners did not want that step. So the toilet was hung on the wall and the run buried inside a thickened wall, the floor left flat from end to end — and the depth still remaining inside that wall is exactly what the niches and the mirror cabinet were set into. Only when the equipment retreats out of sight and the protruding volumes are drawn back can the view begin to unfold.

After the Dining Table Was Removed

The shared space gave up its dining table. The area once claimed by function was released — walking got looser, staying got longer, the gaze grew calmer.

Emptiness is not nothing. It is an arrangement of proportion, keeping room for daily life to breathe.

Three Objects, One Narrative

The aquarium, the stone wall, and the bar are threaded into a single narrative — transparency, thickness, gathering — unfolding in sequence, read from the entry all the way to the kitchen.

The aquarium is visible from three sides; the first glance at the entry reads transparency and depth. The point of the stone wall is not its curve but how its thickness was made — a three-dimensional turning surface that guides and buffers the eye upon entering. The steel plate is not there to echo the stone; it is the bed beneath it — one plate, forming the ledge that carries the laser TV unit on one side and the whole upper sheet of stone on the other, so that face needs one joint less.

The bar is not a straight line. Straight, four people would sit in a row, each looking their own way; instead both ends are cut back on a diagonal and the middle carries a curve, so the four gather around one table. The cooktop is tucked inside the bar, the aquarium's plumbing hidden in the cabinetry, the light wall's access panel placed where no one looks.

"Clean is not surface minimalism. It is complexity, organized in advance."

If you had to remove one thing from your home right now, what would it be?
