A Straight Bar Seats Four People in a Row

The shape of a counter decides how people sit at it.

The common area of this home has no dining table.

Not an oversight — a decision. The ground the table would have taken went to a bar instead, and still gave back a stretch of floor to walk through and stop in.

Start With How It Fails

If the bar were straight, four people would sit in a row, each looking their own way. Everyone present, nobody on the same thing. That is not a furniture problem; it is a shape problem — a straight line offers exactly one way to sit.

The Diagonals Turn People Inward

So it is not a straight line. Both ends are cut back on a diagonal, and the middle carries a curve. The diagonals turn people inward; the curve catches them at the centre.

Four people gather around one table rather than occupying four seats. The counter is no larger — what opens up is the middle. What is gained is not surface area but the relation between the people at it.

Beside the bar sits the aquarium, visible from three sides, its plumbing buried in the cabinetry; the cooktop is tucked inside the bar itself. The less that has to appear on the counter, the more it reads as a table and the less as a set of equipment.

What the Table Gave Back

Where the dining table had stood, there is now a steel shelving unit and a single chair. Once ground is given back by function, movement loosens and people stay on it longer.

Deciding what a home will not hold is usually harder than deciding what it will.

"The counter is no larger. What opens up is the middle."

That dining table in your home — how many times a year does it actually fill up?
