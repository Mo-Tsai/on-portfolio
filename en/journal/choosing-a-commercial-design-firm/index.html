Choosing a Commercial Design Firm: Eight Questions for the First Meeting

Portfolios show what a designer drew. Contracts show what a designer answers for.

When you look for a commercial design firm in Taipei, a portfolio can only tell you what they have drawn. What is worth asking at the first meeting is what they answer for when something goes wrong. These eight questions are useful whoever you end up working with, and we answer them ourselves.

1. Are the venues they built still trading?

Photographs are taken on opening day. Operation only starts after it. If you can, visit one of their venues and sit through a peak service. Our project pages state the business type, floor area and completion year so that you can compare in just this way.

2. Have they done your kind of business, at your scale?

A small open-kitchen restaurant and one of a hundred ping or more are different jobs, not only in size but in what has to be decided first. Ask which of their projects is closest to yours, and where it is the same and where it differs.

3. How far do the drawings in the design fee go?

A floor plan only, and a full set with elevations, ceilings, services and details, are two different deliverables that are often quoted under the same name. Ask them to list the drawings the design fee will deliver.

4. How is the fee split?

The design fee buys judgment, the construction cost pays the trades and suppliers, and the supervision fee buys delivery on site. Read the three separately. Our supervision fee is 12% of construction cost. Commercial projects are always quoted individually, because pricing by floor area distorts. For repeat venues of a brand we charge only a drawing-adaptation fee.

Every firm works differently. What matters is that they can say what each line corresponds to. Details are in what design costs per ping.

5. How are revisions and variations handled?

Our approach: two revisions in the concept stage and two in the 3D drawing stage. Once confirmed, a reversal or an item outside the original scope is quoted separately. Whether that boundary is clear directly affects the cost of communication later. How variations usually arise, and how to plan for them, is in how to set a restaurant fit-out budget.

The stages and proportions of staged payments should also be written into the contract. We invoice by project stage, with proportions and timing agreed in the contract.

6. Who is on site during construction?

Handing over to the contractor after design, and supervising through to completion, are two different services. The first is cheaper, but the risk of deviation is yours.

A circulation route that works on paper fails the moment a joiner builds a unit five centimetres deeper than drawn, and nobody will tell you about those five centimetres unless someone is on site watching. We keep construction supervision inside the process rather than handing it off.

7. Once the drawings leave the office, can someone else read them?

A set of working drawings is read by many people: joiners, metalworkers, suppliers, often people who have never visited the site and will not call to ask. Ask to see an actual drawing: the elevations and details, which dimensions are fixed and which are marked as confirmed on site. How we draw is covered in the drawing-method essays in our Journal.

Ask about rights over the drawings too. With us, once the fee is paid, the copyright in the design belongs to the owner, and the studio keeps the right to show the work in its portfolio, website and award entries.

8. What do they ask you first?

Asking first what style you like, and asking first about your floor area, budget, opening date and lease, are two different starting points. Our contact page asks anyone writing in for the type of business, location and floor area, budget, target opening date and lease status, plus average spend and seats for food and beverage. Then we tell you whether it can be done and roughly where it will get stuck.

A firm that can tell you at the first meeting where it will get stuck is worth more than one that says anything is possible.

It is also where the name ON comes from: we handle both Operation and Narrative, whether a space can be used and whether it is remembered. The full explanation is in ON Stands for Two Words.
