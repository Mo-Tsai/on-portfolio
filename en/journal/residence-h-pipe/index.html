What You Cannot Move Decides Everything You Can

What you cannot move decides everything you can.

By the time we took this project on, the pre-handover layout changes were already done.

The plan was fixed, but the proportions had not followed: one bedroom had been squeezed too small, and its relationship to the study and the bathroom would not stand. A small room is not always the room's fault — usually the one next to it has taken its space.

What Had to Move Was the Bathroom

So what had to move was not the bedroom but the bathroom. Shifting the bathroom entirely is what let the bedroom grow back to the size it needed and the study return to a reasonable scale. Everything the common area later gained begins here.

The Difficulty Was Never the Walls

The difficulty of that move was never the walls; it was the pipe. The soil stack is fixed and cannot be moved, so once the toilet shifted, the horizontal run had to find somewhere to go.

Along the floor would have been simplest, at the cost of raising the level — a threshold to step over for the rest of your life, and that step was exactly what the owners did not want. So the toilet was hung on the wall, the run buried inside the wall and returned to the original stack within it, leaving the floor flat from end to end.

The Depth Left Inside the Wall

Hanging the toilet means thickening the wall; that is what this solution costs. But once the pipework was in, depth still remained inside that wall.

The niche and mirror cabinet were set into it — the cabinet no longer stands on the floor; its width is absorbed by the wall. More is put away, and the room looks larger for it. A solution forced by a constraint ended up being the most usable thing in the room.

"A pipe that cannot move decides whether the whole home is good to live in."

If the spec sheet were in front of you right now, would you read the fittings first, or where the walls stand?
