One Home, Three Volumes

A door is a decision about where the room ends.

The most important decision in this home was to remove a door.

The Door That Disappeared

The kitchen once had a back door. A visible door divides a space into here and elsewhere — the eye travels to it and stops. We let it disappear into a single continuous surface: no handle, no frame, no exit. When it is closed, the wall is whole. Living room, dining room and kitchen became one thing.

Warm Grey, One Step Deeper at a Time

The home is written in gradations of warm grey. The ceiling holds the lightest tone; the walls, in a hand-applied specialty finish, carry a trace of beige; the floor sits one step deeper. Pale leather, textile and wood all fall on the same line.

The walls are not flat. The finish is laid on layer by layer, and in raking light you can read the faint movement of the hand that put it there — lighter than a texture, closer to the weave of cloth. The wall never goes dead.

All the darkness is gathered into a single backlit stone wall. Its veining reads like the shadow of trees at night; light comes through from behind, giving one wall both weight and transparency. A wall that emits light stops being only a wall — the boundary blurs, and the room feels larger than it is, because the eye cannot tell where the wall ends and the light begins. The dining table answers it with a dark stone top: one surface is penetrated by light, the other absorbs it.

Grain That Jumps

An island is the heaviest object in a kitchen, and it is seen from every side. So we cut angles into it, to make it look less heavy than it is. The marble veining runs halfway across each facet and starts again on the next — walk around it and the grain jumps. You will not notice it directly, but you will feel that the thing is moving.

"A home does not need many languages. It needs one theme stated accurately, and three different volumes."

In the living room light illuminates stone; in the dining room it passes through; in the bedroom it retreats to the edges. A home does not need many languages. It needs one theme stated accurately, and three different volumes.

The best design is the kind you walk into and never notice.
